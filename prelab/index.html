Lab safety: what keeps you in the lab, and in one piece

About twenty minutes. Read this before your first lab, try the dress check, then walk the 3D lab and find what's wrong. The graded Lab Safety quiz is on Falcon Online.

You will not be admitted to lab without the right protective equipment. Make-up labs are not guaranteed. You also need a score of 70% or higher on the Lab Safety quiz before you can work in the lab room. If you don't follow the safety rules, you can be removed from the lab and get a zero for that experiment.

1Dress for the lab before you leave home

Your clothes are part of your protection. Chemical splashes land on arms, legs and feet, and long hair can sweep into a flame. Here is what counts as acceptable personal protective equipment (PPE).

Safety eyewear

Good: Z87 safety rating, colorless lenses, fits well over glasses if you wear them.

Not OK: ordinary glasses, sunglasses, colored lenses, poor fit over glasses.

State and federal law require approved eye protection for anyone working in a lab. Buy your own approved pair, even if you wear glasses. Goggles go over your eyes the whole time, not on your forehead.

Top

Good: long sleeves that reach the wrist (a lab jacket, a hoodie), covering front and back from the shoulders to the upper thigh, fits well, no holes.

Not OK: short sleeves, too tight, too short, leaves the back, shoulders or midsection uncovered, has holes.

Cotton or cotton blends are best: they are reasonably fire resistant.

Pants, skirt or dress

Good: ankle length, reasonably loose, no holes, meets the top with no gap.

Not OK: capris, shorts, short skirts, leggings, skinny jeans, holes, a gap at the waist.

Shoes

Good: closed toe and heel, covering the entire top of the foot, such as athletic shoes or boots.

Not OK: sandals, open toe or heel, ballerina flats, boat shoes, Crocs.

Hair

Good: shoulder-length or longer hair pulled back and fastened with a secure tie (a scrunchie works).

Not OK: long hair loose. It is very flammable and can sweep into a beaker or a burner flame.

2The ground rules

These are the standards for acceptable conduct in every Daytona State chemistry lab. They are short because each one comes from a real way people get hurt.

  1. No food or drink. Don't bring anything to eat or drink into the lab, not even with a lid. Food and drink are easily contaminated.
  2. No smoking or vaping. A lit cigarette or a vape is an ignition and battery source near flammable vapors.
  3. Goggles on, always, the whole time you are in the lab, even when you are only writing.
  4. No horseplay or practical jokes. They can cause serious injury.
  5. Only the authorized experiment. Follow the procedure in order. Any change needs your instructor's OK.
  6. Nothing in, nothing out. Don't bring apparatus or reagents into the lab, and don't take any out.
  7. Report every injury, even small cuts and minor chemical or heat burns, to your instructor right away.
  8. Backpacks off the floor. Bags go on the tables or in the cubbies under the benches, never in the aisles.
  9. Stay out of the stockroom. It is a restricted area.
  10. Wash your hands before you leave, so you don't carry contamination out with you.

3Know where the safety equipment is

In an emergency you won't have time to look around. Learn where these are on your first day. In the 3D lab, every one of them is clickable.

Eyewash

At the safety station in the corner. Push the paddle, hold your eyelids open with your fingers, and flush for at least 15 minutes. Keep the counter around it clear.

Safety shower

Above the safety station. Stand under it and pull the yellow triangle down. Use it for a large chemical splash on your body or for clothing on fire. Never block the area under it.

Fire blanket

In the bright orange bag on the safety station shelf. Use it to smother a clothing fire after stop, drop and roll.

First-aid kit

On the lower shelf of the safety station. For minor cuts and burns, and you still report every injury.

Fire extinguisher

In the cabinet by the stockroom door. P.A.S.S.: Pull the pin, Aim at the base, Squeeze, Sweep. Only for small fires, only if trained, and only with a clear exit behind you.

Gas and power shutoffs

The gas shutoff valves and the red emergency power cutoff are for your instructor to use in a gas leak or electrical emergency.

Exits and the procedures chart

Know where both doors are. The emergency procedures flip chart by the door covers fire, severe weather, evacuation, first aid and hurricanes.

Spill kit

Absorbent, sand and acid neutralizer. Alert people nearby and tell your instructor before anyone cleans up a spill.

4Working with chemicals and heat

Read the label twice

Names can be one letter apart (potassium nitrate vs. potassium nitrite), and the concentration matters too. The wrong bottle can make a reaction run much faster than expected.

Take only what you need

Measure out only the amount the procedure calls for. Never pour extra back into the original bottle; it can contaminate everyone's reagent.

Acid into water

When diluting an acid or base, pour it slowly into the water, never water into acid (A to W). Adding water to concentrated acid can boil and splatter.

Use the fume hood

Work with gases, vapors or smells in the hood. Keep the sash at the marked working height (about 18 in), and close it when the hood is not in use.

Hot equipment

The single biggest source of lab injuries. Hot plates, burners, and the glassware on them stay hot far longer than you think, and hot glass looks just like cold glass. Never leave a hot plate on and unattended.

Flammables cabinet

Flammable liquids live inside the yellow cabinet with the doors closed and latched. Nothing gets stored on top of it.

5Read the hazards: labels, safety data sheets and signs

Every chemical tells you how it can hurt you and what to do about it, if you know where to look. Look at three places before you open a bottle: the label, the safety data sheet (SDS), and any hazard sign on the door or the cabinet.

The label

Acetone

DANGER

Highly flammable liquid and vapor. Causes serious eye irritation. May cause drowsiness or dizziness.

Keep away from heat, sparks and open flames. Keep container tightly closed. Use only in a well-ventilated area. IF IN EYES: rinse cautiously with water for several minutes.

  5. Precautions. What to do about it. "Use only in a well-ventilated area" means in the fume hood in our lab. "Keep away from heat" means no hot plate or flame nearby.

The safety data sheet (SDS)

Every chemical in the lab has an SDS (older ones are called MSDS). It always has the same 16 sections in the same order, so you can find what you need fast. The SDS binder is kept in the lab manager's prep room (the stockroom door next to the fire extinguisher); ask your instructor if you need one. You can also look one up online by the chemical's name. The sections you'll use most:

Section 2: Hazards

The same pictograms, signal word and hazard statements as the label.

Section 4: First aid

What to do for eye or skin contact, breathing it in or swallowing it.

Section 5: Fire fighting

Which extinguishing methods work, and which ones, such as water, must not be used.

Section 7: Handling and storage

Where it is stored (for example, the flammables cabinet) and what to keep it away from.

Section 8: Exposure controls and PPE

Whether you need the fume hood, gloves or other protection.

The hazard diamond on doors and cabinets

  • Blue: health hazard.
  • Red: flammability.
  • Yellow: instability, how easily it reacts.
  • White: special hazards. A W with a line through it means do not use water; OX means oxidizer.
  • Numbers run from 0 (minimal) to 4 (severe).

When water helps, and when it makes things worse

SituationWater?What to do
Chemical in your eyes or on your skinYesEyewash or safety shower for at least 15 minutes, unless the SDS says otherwise.
Clothing on fireYesStop, drop and roll; fire blanket or safety shower.
Burning organic solvent in a beakerNoWater can splash the burning liquid and spread it. Turn off the hot plate if you can, cover the beaker to smother it, and tell your instructor.
Reactive metals such as sodium or potassium (W with a line)NoThey react violently with water. Leave it to your instructor.
Fire in plugged-in electrical equipmentNoWater conducts electricity. Tell your instructor; the emergency power cutoff is theirs to use.

When in doubt, check Section 5 of the SDS and the white quarter of the diamond, and tell your instructor before anything else.

6Waste and cleanup

Chemical waste

Use the waste containers provided. Organic waste goes in the bottle in the fume hood: lift the red funnel lid, pour slowly, close the lid. Pouring onto a closed lid makes a spill.

Never down the sink

Organic solvents and other chemicals do not go down the drain. They can be flammable and pollute the water. If you're unsure, ask your instructor.

Broken glass

Into the blue and white broken glass container, never the wastebasket. Sweep it up with a brush and dustpan, not your hands, and tell your instructor.

Wastebasket

Only solid, non-hazardous items like litmus paper, matches and filter paper.

Leave it clean

Clean your glassware, sponge off the bench and the shelves above it, dry with a paper towel, rinse reagent bottles before returning them, and brush any spilled powder off the balance.

7If something goes wrong

Chemical in your eyes

Get to the eyewash, hold your eyes open, and flush for at least 15 minutes. Shout for help so your instructor knows.

Large splash on your body

Get under the safety shower, pull the handle, remove contaminated clothing, and rinse for 15 minutes.

Clothing on fire

Stop, drop and roll. Smother the flames with the fire blanket, or get under the safety shower. Never run: it feeds the fire.

Spill

Warn the people around you, keep everyone out of it, and tell your instructor right away.

Any injury

Cuts, burns, anything: report it to your instructor immediately, however small. For a medical emergency, call 911 (9-911 from a campus phone).
